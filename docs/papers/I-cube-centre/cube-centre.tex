side $2$ is optimal for $2$ to $2^d$ unit cubes, with proofs checked in Lean}

\date{8 November 2026; revised 10 November 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Let $s_d(n)$ be the smallest side of a $d$-dimensional cube that holds $n$ unit $d$-cubes with disjoint interiors, each placed by an arbitrary rigid motion. We prove $s_d(n)=2$ for every $2\le n\le 2^d$ and $d\in\{3,4,5\}$, and $s_d(k^d)=k$ in every dimension. All proofs are formalized in Lean~4 with Mathlib and use only the standard axioms. The lower bound $s_d(2)\ge 2$ is the \emph{cube centre lemma}: a unit $d$-cube inside a cube of side less than $2$ contains the container's centre in its interior. Following Hosono, it reduces to a matrix inequality, Lemma~F: $\sum_i \abs{R_{ij}}\,(2-\norm{R_i}_1)\le 1$ for every orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix $R$ and every column $j$. Hosono proved Lemma~F for $d\le 6$ with computer-assisted far regions. We give proofs whose only computation is checked by Lean. In dimension~3, a minimiser argument on a circle reduces the inequality to an algebraic identity. In dimension~4, Hosono's near-axis step is combined with a new far-region bound from zero-sum vectors and Cauchy--Schwarz. In dimension~5 the near-axis step and the far-region bound leave a gap, the band $9/20\le c^2\le 3/4$ for the largest entry $c$ of the column. We close it with a new bound that pairs each zero-sum vector with its own column, turned in a fixed plane, and with four sphere inequalities certified by 1174 interval cases. Every statement in this paper is linked to its Lean declaration. Paper~IV of this series treats every dimension.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The problem}
Pack $n$ unit cubes into a cube, as small as possible. Each unit cube may be rotated, reflected and translated; distinct cubes must have disjoint interiors. In dimension $d$ write
\[
s_d(n)=\inf\{s\ge0:\ n\text{ unit $d$-cubes fit in }[0,s]^d\}.
\]
The $2\times2\times\dots\times2$ grid gives $s_d(n)\le 2$ for $n\le 2^d$, and the volume bound gives $s_d(n)\ge n^{1/d}$, which is sharp only at $n=2^d$. Whether $s_d(n)=2$ already for $n=2$ is a question about a single cube: \emph{can a unit cube inside a cube of side $s<2$ avoid the container's centre?} If it cannot, two such cubes overlap at the centre, and $s_d(n)=2$ for every $n$ from $2$ to $2^d$ (\cref{fig:grid}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig_grid_tilt.pdf}
\caption{Left: the grid packing of $2^3$ unit cubes in a cube of side $2$. Right: a tilted unit cube in a cube of side $s<2$, pushed into the corner of the region it can reach; the container's centre (red) is still inside it. The cube centre lemma says this always happens, so no two unit cubes fit at side $s<2$.}
\label{fig:grid}
\end{figure}

For squares ($d=2$) the answer is elementary and classical. In higher dimensions it is not: a rotated cube can be much thinner along some axes than along others, and the region it can reach is a box whose corners are far from the centre. Hiroaki Hosono~\cite{hosono} reduced the question to an inequality for orthogonal matrices, which we call Lemma~F after his \code{PROOF.md}, and proved it with computer assistance for $d\le 6$.

\subsection{Main results}

\begin{maintheorem}
For $d\in\{3,4,5\}$ and every $2\le n\le 2^d$, the smallest cube holding $n$ unit $d$-cubes has side exactly $2$. In every dimension $d\ge1$ and for every $k\ge1$, the smallest cube holding $k^d$ unit $d$-cubes has side exactly $k$. The results hold for placements by arbitrary rigid motions, reflections included, and require only disjoint interiors.
\end{maintheorem}

\leanbox{\lean{CubeCentre}{cubincub_plateau} ($d=3$), \lean{Tesseracts}{c8inc8_plateau} ($d=4$), \lean{Penteracts}{hc5inhc5_plateau} ($d=5$), \lean{Cubes}{cubes_in_cube_optimal} ($k^d$ cubes).}

The plateau theorems rest on the cube centre lemma in each dimension (\lean{CubeCentre}{centre3}, \lean{Tesseracts}{centre4}, \lean{Penteracts}{centre5}), and these on Lemma~F (\cref{sec:lemmaF}). \Cref{tab:results} lists the 55 catalogue records~\cite{archive} that these theorems prove optimal. Paper~II~\cite{widthball} proves thirty further records with other pieces and containers, and Paper~III~\cite{patterns} places all of them among the structural patterns of the catalogue.

\subsection{What is new}
The plateau argument and the reduction to Lemma~F are Hosono's, and so is the near-axis step used in dimensions~4 and~5, which we use in his own Lean formalization. The following are new:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Dimension 3} (\cref{sec:d3}). A proof of Lemma~F without computer search. A frame rotation on a circle has a minimiser; at a minimiser some entry vanishes, and with a zero entry the inequality is an algebraic identity.
\item \textbf{Dimension 4} (\cref{sec:d4}). An elementary far region: zero-sum vectors and Cauchy--Schwarz reduce Lemma~F to a separable inequality in the entries of one column, closed by three line bounds and 81 linear cases.
\item \textbf{Dimension 5} (\cref{sec:d5}). In dimension~5 the near-axis step needs $c^2\ge3/4$ and the elementary far bound needs $c^2\le 1/2$, where $c$ is the largest entry of the column. We close the band between them with a rotated weight (\cref{thm:band}), and reduce what remains to sphere inequalities for $\sum_i(s_i^3+\lambda s_i)$. Their proofs are 1174 interval cases, generated from exact certificates and checked by Lean.
\end{enumerate}
The Lean proofs use no computer search beyond the generated certificates, which Lean checks line by line.

\section{Packings, the common-point lemma and the volume bound}\label{sec:setting}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{This section.} The definition of a packing, the common-point lemma, the volume bound, and the plateau theorem that turns a centre lemma into optimality for $2\le n\le2^d$.\\
\textbf{Records.} The five volume records: $k^3$ cubes ($k=2,3,4$), $16$ tesseracts, $32$ five-cubes.\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{Basic}, \leanfile{Cubes}.
\end{summarybox}

\subsection{Packings in Lean}
Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space, $K,C\subseteq E$ and $s\ge 0$.

\begin{definition}[\lean{Basic}{Packing}]
A \emph{packing} of $n$ copies of $K$ in $sC$ is a family of affine isometries $g_1,\dots,g_n$ of $E$ with $g_i(K)\subseteq sC$ for all $i$ and $\interior g_i(K)\cap\interior g_j(K)=\emptyset$ for $i\ne j$.
\end{definition}

Affine isometries include rotations, reflections and translations, so every lower bound proved for this definition holds a fortiori for the catalogue's packings, which use rotations only. Touching is allowed. A scale $s$ is \emph{optimal} for $n$ pieces if a packing at scale $s$ exists and every packing has scale at least $s$; in Lean this is the conjunction \texttt{Nonempty (Packing K C s n) $\wedge$ $\forall t\ge0$, Packing K C t n $\to$ s $\le$ t}.

\begin{lemma}[Restriction; \lean{Basic}{Packing.restrict}]
A packing of $n$ copies restricts to a packing of any $m\le n$ of them. Hence $s(m)\le s(n)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Common point; \lean{Basic}{no_packing_two_of_common_point}]\label{lem:common}
If every congruent copy of $K$ inside $sC$ contains a fixed point $c$ in its interior, then no two copies fit in $sC$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Two copies would both contain $c$ in their interiors.
\end{proof}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The volume bound and $k^d$ cubes}{The volume bound and grids of cubes}}

\begin{theorem}[Volume bound; \lean{Basic}{card_mul_volume_le}]
If $K$ is convex and $n$ copies of $K$ pack in $sC$, then $n\,\vol(K)\le \abs{s}^{\dim E}\vol(C)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Affine isometries preserve Lebesgue measure (\lean{Basic}{affineIsometry_measurePreserving}), and the boundary of a convex set is null (\lean{Basic}{volume_interior_of_convex}). The interiors are disjoint subsets of $sC$.
\end{proof}

Let $Q_d=[0,1]^d$ (\lean{Cubes}{cube}). The grid of $k^d$ translates $v+Q_d$, $v\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}^d$, is a packing in $kQ_d$ (\lean{Cubes}{gridPacking}); distinct cells are separated by a coordinate hyperplane (\lean{Cubes}{cell_disjoint}).

\begin{corollary}[\lean{Cubes}{cubes_in_cube_optimal}]
For every $d\ge1$ and $k\ge1$, side $k$ is optimal for $k^d$ unit $d$-cubes.
\end{corollary}

This gives the five volume records of \cref{tab:results}. For $n<k^d$ the volume bound is not sharp, and a different argument is needed.

\subsection{From the centre lemma to a plateau}

\begin{theorem}[Plateau; \lean{Cubes}{cube_plateau_of_centre_lemma}]\label{thm:plateau}
Suppose that for every $0\le s<2$ and every affine isometry $g$ with $g(Q_d)\subseteq sQ_d$, the point $\frac s2\one$ lies in $\interior g(Q_d)$. Then side $2$ is optimal for every $2\le n\le 2^d$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The grid restricted to $n$ cells gives side $2$. If $n\ge 2$ cubes fitted at side $s<2$, the hypothesis and \cref{lem:common} would give a contradiction.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis is the \emph{cube centre lemma in dimension $d$}. The rest of the paper proves it for $d=3,4,5$. The same pattern, a common point and deletion, proves most optimal plateaus in the catalogue (\lean{Relations}{optimal_plateau_of_common_point}; Paper~II~\cite{widthball} gives further instances).

\section{The cube centre lemma and Lemma F}\label{sec:lemmaF}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{This section.} In every dimension, the cube centre lemma follows from Lemma~F, an inequality for orthogonal matrices (Hosono's reduction).\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{CentreGeneral} (\lean{CentreGeneral}{centre_of_lemmaF}).
\end{summarybox}

\subsection{Widths of a rotated cube}
Write an affine isometry as $g(x)=Lx+b$ with $L$ orthogonal, and let $L_{ij}$ be its matrix entries. The image $g(Q_d)$ is the set of points $b+\sum_k t_kLe_k$ with $t\in[0,1]^d$. Its extent in coordinate $i$ is an interval of length
\[
w_i=\sum_k \abs{L_{ik}}=\norm{L_i}_1 ,
\]
the $\ell^1$ norm of row $i$ of $L$. If $g(Q_d)\subseteq[0,s]^d$, this interval lies in $[0,s]$, so the image's centre $m=g(\tfrac12\one)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centre-box}
\Bigl\lvert \tfrac s2-m_i\Bigr\rvert\le \frac{s-w_i}{2}\qquad(i=1,\dots,d).
\end{equation}
The admissible positions of $m$ form a box (\cref{fig:square}). The cube centre lemma asks whether, for every position in this box, the container's centre lies in the cube.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig_square.pdf}
\caption{The two-dimensional picture. A unit square rotated by $\theta$ has width $w=\abs{\cos\theta}+\abs{\sin\theta}$ along each axis. Inside a square of side $s$, its centre ranges over a square of side $s-w$ (green). The container's centre lies inside the rotated square for every admissible position, here shown at two opposite corners.}
\label{fig:square}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Lemma F}

\begin{definition}[\lean{NearAxis}{Fcol}, \lean{NearAxis}{LemmaF}]
For a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix $R$ and a column $j$ let
\[
F_j(R)=\sum_{i=1}^d \abs{R_{ij}}\,\bigl(2-\norm{R_i}_1\bigr).
\]
\emph{Lemma F in dimension $d$} is the statement $F_j(R)\le 1$ for every orthogonal $R$ and every $j$.
\end{definition}

For the identity, $F_j=1$: the inequality is tight. For a rotation by $\theta$ in a coordinate plane, with $\sigma=\abs{\cos\theta}+\abs{\sin\theta}$, the two rows meeting the plane give $F_j=\sigma(2-\sigma)=1-(\sigma-1)^2$. In dimension~2 this is the whole proof of Lemma~F. In higher dimensions $F_j$ can exceed this family's values far from the axes but stays below $1$ (\cref{fig:lemmaF}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_lemmaF.pdf}
\caption{$F_j(R)$ for random orthogonal matrices (Haar samples, and perturbations of signed permutations and of $2\times2$ rotation blocks, where $F$ is largest), plotted against the largest squared entry of column $j$. Red: rotations in one coordinate plane. Shaded: the regions of the proofs in \cref{sec:d4,sec:d5}. Lemma F says every point lies below the dashed line $F=1$.}
\label{fig:lemmaF}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Lemma F gives the centre lemma; \lean{CentreGeneral}{centre_of_lemmaF}]\label{thm:centre-of-F}
If Lemma~F holds in dimension $d$, then for every $0\le s<2$ and every affine isometry $g$ with $g(Q_d)\subseteq sQ_d$, the point $\frac s2\one$ lies in $\interior g(Q_d)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $g(x)=Lx+b$ and $c=\frac s2\one$, and let $y=L^{-1}(c-b)$ be the container's centre in the cube's own coordinates. Then $c\in\interior g(Q_d)$ if and only if $y\in(0,1)^d$. Since $L$ is orthogonal, $y-\frac12\one=L^{\top}(c-m)$, so by \eqref{eq:centre-box}
\[
\Bigl\lvert y_j-\tfrac12\Bigr\rvert=\Bigl\lvert\sum_i L_{ij}(c_i-m_i)\Bigr\rvert\le\sum_i\abs{L_{ij}}\,\frac{s-w_i}2<\sum_i\abs{L_{ij}}\,\frac{2-w_i}2=\frac{F_j(L)}2\le\frac12 .
\]
The strict inequality uses $s<2$ and $\sum_i\abs{L_{ij}}>0$ (column $j$ is a unit vector). Hence $y\in(0,1)^d$.
\end{proof}

In Lean the extreme points of each coordinate interval are images of explicit vertices of the cube, chosen by the signs of row $i$ (\lean{CentreGeneral}{pos_part}, \lean{CentreGeneral}{neg_part}). The same argument appears in dimension~3 as \lean{CubeCentre}{centre3}.

So the cube centre lemma in dimension $d$ follows from Lemma~F in dimension $d$, and \cref{thm:plateau} turns it into an optimal plateau. The next three sections prove Lemma~F for $d=3,4,5$.

\section{Dimension 3: a minimiser argument}\label{sec:d3}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Result.} Lemma~F for $d=3$, hence side $2$ for $2\le n\le8$ cubes.\\
\textbf{Argument.} A frame rotation on a circle has a minimiser; at a minimiser some entry vanishes, and then the inequality is an identity. No computer search.\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{CubeCentre} (\lean{CubeCentre}{core}, \lean{CubeCentre}{centre3}).
\end{summarybox}

For $d=3$ write the columns of $R$ as $a,b,c$. Since $\sum_ia_i^2=1$,
\[
F_1(R)=\sum_i\abs{a_i}\bigl(2-\abs{a_i}-\abs{b_i}-\abs{c_i}\bigr)=2\norm a_1-1-\sum_i\abs{a_i}\bigl(\abs{b_i}+\abs{c_i}\bigr),
\]
so Lemma~F is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:core}
2\bigl(\norm a_1-1\bigr)\le\sum_i\abs{a_i}\bigl(\abs{b_i}+\abs{c_i}\bigr).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Lemma F in dimension 3; \lean{CubeCentre}{core}, \lean{LemmaF4}{lemmaF3}]\label{thm:d3}
Inequality \eqref{eq:core} holds for every orthogonal $3\times3$ matrix with columns $a,b,c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: a zero entry.} Suppose $c_3=0$ (\lean{CubeCentre}{reduced}). Then $(c_1,c_2)$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $(a_1,a_2)$ and $(b_1,b_2)$, so $(a_1,a_2)=\ell(-c_2,c_1)$ and $(b_1,b_2)=m(-c_2,c_1)$ with $\ell^2=b_3^2$ and $m^2=a_3^2$ (from the unit rows and columns). Put $P=\abs{b_3}$, $Q=\abs{a_3}$, $S=\abs{c_2}$, $T=\abs{c_1}$, so $P^2+Q^2=S^2+T^2=1$. Then $\abs{a_1}=PS$, $\abs{a_2}=PT$, $\abs{b_1}=QS$, $\abs{b_2}=QT$, and the difference of the two sides of \eqref{eq:core} is
\[
2(1-P)(1-Q)+2P(1-S)(1-T)\ \ge 0
\]
(\lean{CubeCentre}{pqst}; the identity uses $S^2+T^2=1$). Any other zero entry of $b$ or $c$ reduces to this one by permuting rows and exchanging $b$ and $c$ (\lean{CubeCentre}{zero_case}).

\emph{Step 2: the general case.} Keep $a$ and rotate the pair $(b,c)$ in its own plane: for a unit vector $(p,q)$ put $b'=pb+qc$, $c'=pc-qb$. Then $(a,b',c')$ is again orthonormal, the left side of \eqref{eq:core} is unchanged, and the right side becomes
\[
\Phi(p,q)=\sum_i\abs{a_i}\,T_i(p,q),\qquad T_i(p,q)=\abs{pb_i+qc_i}+\abs{pc_i-qb_i}
\]
(\lean{CubeCentre}{Phi}, \lean{CubeCentre}{T}). $\Phi$ is continuous on the unit circle, so it has a minimiser $(p^*,q^*)$, and $\Phi(1,0)\ge\Phi(p^*,q^*)$. If the frame at the minimiser has a zero entry in $b'$ or $c'$, Step~1 applies to it and gives \eqref{eq:core} for $\Phi(p^*,q^*)$, hence for $\Phi(1,0)$. Otherwise every entry of $b'$ and $c'$ is nonzero, so every $T_i(p^*,q^*)$ is positive and so is the minimum $\Phi(p^*,q^*)$. No entry changes sign for small $t\ge0$, and each $T_i$ is linear there. The two neighbours $(p^*\mp tq^*,\,q^*\pm tp^*)$, which have norm $\sqrt{1+t^2}$, then satisfy
\[
T_i(p^*-tq^*,q^*+tp^*)+T_i(p^*+tq^*,q^*-tp^*)=2\,T_i(p^*,q^*)
\]
(\lean{CubeCentre}{T_pair}). Rescaled to the circle by the homogeneity of $T_i$ (\lean{CubeCentre}{T_hom}), the two neighbours average to $\Phi(p^*,q^*)/\sqrt{1+t^2}$, which is less than the positive minimum. This is impossible, so the minimiser always has a zero entry, and \eqref{eq:core} holds.
\end{proof}

Hosono had proved \cref{thm:d3} with an interval computation. The cube centre lemma in dimension~3 is \lean{CubeCentre}{centre3}, and \lean{CubeCentre}{cubincub_plateau} gives $s_3(n)=2$ for $2\le n\le 8$.

\section{The near-axis step}\label{sec:near}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Result.} Near a coordinate axis ($c^2\ge1-1/m$), Lemma~F in dimension $m+1$ follows from Lemma~F in dimension $m$, for $m\le4$.\\
\textbf{Origin.} Hosono's argument and Lean file, used unchanged (MIT licence).\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{NearAxis} (\lean{NearAxis}{near_identity}, \lean{NearAxis}{near_region}).
\end{summarybox}

The near-axis step is Hosono's~\cite{hosono}. We use his Lean file unchanged except for its namespace (\leanfile{NearAxis}, MIT licence), and describe it here because dimensions~4 and~5 both depend on it. It reduces Lemma~F in dimension $m+1$, near a coordinate axis, to Lemma~F in dimension $m$.

\subsection{Block form}
Fix a column $j$ and an entry $c=R_{i_0j}$. Deleting row $i_0$ and column $j$ writes $R$ in block form (\cref{fig:block}):
\[
R\simeq\begin{pmatrix} c & r^\top\\ x & M\end{pmatrix},\qquad c\in\R,\ r,x\in\R^m,\ M\in\R^{m\times m},
\]
where $r$ is the rest of row $i_0$ and $x$ the rest of column $j$ (\lean{LemmaF4}{block_orth}, \lean{LemmaF4}{Fcol_eq_F1}). Then
\[
F_j(R)=F_1(c,r,x,M)=\abs c\,(2-\abs c-\norm r_1)+\sum_{i=1}^m\abs{x_i}\,(2-\abs{x_i}-\norm{M_i}_1)
\]
(\lean{NearAxis}{F1}). The orthonormality relations used are collected in \lean{NearAxis}{BlockOrth}: column $j$ and row $i_0$ are unit vectors, column $j$ is orthogonal to the other columns, and the rows other than $i_0$ are orthonormal.

\subsection{The polar factor}
For $c>0$ and $x\ne0$ put $Q=(I+\beta xx^\top)M$ with $\beta=(1/c-1)/\norm x_2^2$ (\lean{NearAxis}{polarQ}). This is the orthogonal factor of the polar decomposition $M=PQ$, $P=I-\frac{1-c}{\norm x^2}xx^\top=I-\frac{1}{1+c}xx^\top$, and it is orthogonal (\lean{NearAxis}{polarQ_orth}). Two facts follow from the orthogonality of column $j$ to the others. First, $M^\top x=-cr$ and $M^\top x=c\,Q^\top x$, so $y:=Q^\top x=-r$. Second, the rows of $M$ are those of $Q$ moved by a rank-one term,
\[
M_i=Q_i-\tfrac{1}{1+c}\,x_i\,y^\top .
\]

\begin{lemma}[\lean{NearAxis}{near_identity}]\label{lem:near-identity}
With $K(Q,x)=\norm{Q^\top x}_1+\sum_i\abs{x_i}\norm{Q_i}_1-2\norm x_1$ (\lean{NearAxis}{K}),
\[
1-F_1(c,r,x,M)\ \ge\ K(Q,x)+2(1-c)\bigl(1-\norm{Q^\top x}_1\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By the triangle inequality $\norm{M_i}_1\ge\norm{Q_i}_1-\frac{1}{1+c}\abs{x_i}\norm y_1$, and $\norm r_1=\norm y_1$. Substituting into $F_1$ and using $\frac{1}{1+c}\norm x_2^2=1-c$ gives
$1-F_1\ge 2(1-c)+(2c-1)\norm y_1-\sum_i\abs{x_i}(2-\norm{Q_i}_1)$, which is the claim, because $K(Q,x)=\norm y_1-\sum_i\abs{x_i}(2-\norm{Q_i}_1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lemma K; \lean{NearAxis}{lemmaK}]
If $Q$ is orthogonal, every row has $\norm{Q_i}_1\le2$, and every column has $F_j(Q)\le1$, then $K(Q,x)\ge0$ for all $x$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
With $y=Q^\top x$ we have $x=Qy$, so $\abs{x_i}\le\sum_j\abs{Q_{ij}}\abs{y_j}$. Since $2-\norm{Q_i}_1\ge0$,
\[
\sum_i\abs{x_i}(2-\norm{Q_i}_1)\le\sum_j\abs{y_j}\sum_i\abs{Q_{ij}}(2-\norm{Q_i}_1)=\sum_j\abs{y_j}F_j(Q)\le\norm y_1. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

For $m\le4$ every unit vector has $\norm v_1\le\sqrt m\,\norm v_2\le2$ (\lean{NearAxis}{rowL1_le_two}), and $m\norm x_2^2\le1$ gives $\norm{Q^\top x}_1\le\sqrt m\norm x_2\le1$ (\lean{NearAxis}{l1_QTx_le_one}). Together:

\begin{theorem}[Near region; \lean{NearAxis}{near_region}]\label{thm:near}
Let $m\le4$ and assume Lemma~F in dimension $m$. If $c>0$ and $c^2\ge1-1/m$, then $F_1(c,r,x,M)\le1$.
\end{theorem}

The restriction $m\le 4$ enters only through $\norm{Q_i}_1\le2$. For $m=3$ the near region is $c^2\ge2/3$, and for $m=4$ it is $c^2\ge3/4$. A negative $c$ is handled by changing the sign of row $i_0$, which changes neither $F$ nor the relations (\lean{LemmaF4}{near4}, \lean{LemmaF5}{near5}).

\section{Dimension 4: an elementary far region}\label{sec:d4}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Result.} Lemma~F for $d=4$, hence side $2$ for $2\le n\le16$ tesseracts.\\
\textbf{Argument.} Near the axes, the near-axis step with $m=3$. Elsewhere, new: zero-sum vectors and Cauchy--Schwarz give a separable inequality, closed by three line bounds and $81$ linear cases.\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{LemmaF4} (\lean{LemmaF4}{pair_bound}, \lean{LemmaF4}{far4}, \lean{LemmaF4}{lemmaF4}), \leanfile{Tesseracts}.
\end{summarybox}

Fix a column $j$ of an orthogonal $4\times4$ matrix $R$, and write $x_i=\abs{R_{ij}}$ and $S=\sum_ix_i$. If some $x_i^2\ge2/3$, \cref{thm:near} with $m=3$ and \cref{thm:d3} give $F_j\le1$ (\lean{LemmaF4}{near4}). It remains to treat columns with every $x_i^2\le 2/3$.

\subsection{Zero-sum vectors}

\begin{lemma}[\lean{LemmaF4}{pair_bound}]\label{lem:pair}
Let $a,b$ be orthogonal, with $\norm b_2=1$. Then $\sqrt2\sum_i\abs{a_i}\,b_i^2\le\sum_i\abs{a_i}\abs{b_i}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The vector $z_i=a_ib_i$ has zero sum. For a zero-sum vector the positive and negative parts have equal $\ell^1$ norm, so $\norm z_1^2\ge 2\norm z_2^2$. By Cauchy--Schwarz with the unit vector $(\abs{b_i})_i$, $\norm z_2\ge\sum_i\abs{z_i}\abs{b_i}=\sum_i\abs{a_i}b_i^2$.
\end{proof}

Lean uses $140/99\le\sqrt2$ in place of $\sqrt2$. Apply \cref{lem:pair} with $a$ the column $j$ and $b$ each other column, and sum. The rows are unit vectors, so $\sum_{k\ne j}R_{ik}^2=1-x_i^2$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:far4}
F_j(R)=2S-1-\sum_{k\ne j}\sum_i x_i\abs{R_{ik}}\ \le\ 2S-1-\sqrt2\sum_i x_i\bigl(1-x_i^2\bigr)
\end{equation}
(\lean{LemmaF4}{far_core}). Since $\sum_ix_i^2=1$, the right side is at most $1$ exactly when
\[
\sum_i\varphi(x_i)\ge-2,\qquad\varphi(t)=\sqrt2\,(t-t^3)-2t.
\]

\subsection{Line bounds}
The function $\varphi$ is not convex in $t^2$, so we bound it from below by lines in $t^2$ on three ranges (\cref{fig:phi4}):
\begin{align*}
\varphi(t)&\ge-0.06592-1.65782\,t^2 && (t^2\le2/5), &&\text{\lean{LemmaF4}{phi_small}}\\
\varphi(t)&\ge\phantom{-}0.03645-1.91374\,t^2 && (2/5\le t^2\le3/5), &&\text{\lean{LemmaF4}{phi_large2}}\\
\varphi(t)&\ge-0.14366-1.65782\,t^2 && (2/5\le t^2\le2/3), &&\text{\lean{LemmaF4}{phi_large}}
\end{align*}
Each is a one-variable cubic inequality on an interval with an explicit nonnegativity certificate. Every coordinate falls in one of three ranges (\lean{LemmaF4}{phi_cases}); for each of the $3^4=81$ combinations, the sum of the bounds is linear in the $x_i^2$ and is closed by \code{linarith} using $\sum_ix_i^2=1$ (\lean{LemmaF4}{far_poly}). The true minimum of $\sum_i\varphi(x_i)$ on the far region is about $0.009$ above $-2$, attained at $x^2=(2/3,1/9,1/9,1/9)$.

\caption{The far region in dimension~4. Left: $\varphi(t)=\frac{140}{99}(t-t^3)-2t$ against $t^2$. Right: the gaps between $\varphi$ and its three line bounds, each nonnegative on its range.}

\begin{theorem}[Lemma F in dimension 4; \lean{LemmaF4}{lemmaF4}]\label{thm:d4}
$F_j(R)\le1$ for every orthogonal $4\times4$ matrix and every column $j$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If some entry of column $j$ has square at least $2/3$, use \lean{LemmaF4}{near4}; otherwise \lean{LemmaF4}{far4}.
\end{proof}

With \cref{thm:centre-of-F} this gives the cube centre lemma \lean{Tesseracts}{centre4} and the plateau \lean{Tesseracts}{c8inc8_plateau}: side $2$ is optimal for $2$ to $16$ tesseracts. Hosono's proof of the far region uses the spectral bound $\sum_{k}\norm{z^k}_2\ge\tr\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top}$, $p_i=x_i^2$, which is stronger and needs a computer to certify; the weaker bound \eqref{eq:far4} suffices in dimension~4.

\section{Dimension 5: the band}\label{sec:d5}

\begin{summarybox}
\textbf{Result.} Lemma~F for $d=5$, hence side $2$ for $2\le n\le32$ five-cubes.\\
\textbf{Argument.} Near region ($c^2\ge3/4$): the near-axis step with $m=4$. Far region ($c^2\le9/20$): the zero-sum bound with five rows. The band between them is new: a rotated weight pairs each zero-sum vector with its own column, and four sphere inequalities, certified by $1174$ interval cases, close it.\\
\textbf{Lean.} \leanfile{LemmaF5}, \leanfile{LemmaF5Sphere} (generated), \leanfile{Penteracts}.
\end{summarybox}

\subsection{Three regions}
In dimension~5 both previous arguments fall short. Let $c$ be an entry of column $j$. \Cref{thm:near} with $m=4$ needs $c^2\ge3/4$. The analogue of \eqref{eq:far4} with five rows holds while every entry has square at most $1/2$, with slack about $0.006$ at that boundary, and fails beyond it, for instance at $x^2=(0.55,\,0.1125,\,0.1125,\,0.1125,\,0.1125)$. We split by the entries of the column:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Region & Condition & Argument & Lean \\
\midrule
near & some $c^2\ge3/4$ & polar step, Lemma F in 4D & \lean{LemmaF5}{near5} \\
band & some $9/20\le c^2\le 3/4$ & rotated weight + sphere bounds & \lean{LemmaF5}{band5} \\
far & every $c^2\le 9/20$ & zero-sum bound, five rows & \lean{LemmaF5}{far5} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The band is new. In it, write $R$ in block form around $c$ as in \cref{sec:near}, assume $c>0$ (flip the sign of row $i_0$ otherwise), and put $\rho=\norm x_2=\sqrt{1-c^2}$, $X_i=\abs{x_i}$,
\[
T=\sum_{i=1}^4X_i,\qquad m_3=\sum_{i=1}^4X_i^3 .
\]

\subsection{The rotated weight}
Fix another column $k$ of $R$: it is $(r_k,M_{1k},\dots,M_{4k})$, a unit vector. Its entrywise product with column $j$,
\[
\zeta^{(k)}=(c\,r_k,\ x_1M_{1k},\ \dots,\ x_4M_{4k})\in\R^5,
\]
has zero sum, because the two columns are orthogonal. As in \cref{lem:pair}, $\norm{\zeta}_1\ge\sqrt2\norm\zeta_2\ge\sqrt2\sum_i\abs{\zeta_i}\abs{w_i}$ for every weight $w$ with $\norm w_2\le1$. In \cref{sec:d4} the weight was the column $k$ itself. In the band we turn it first.

Let $\cos\psi=14/25$ and $\sin\psi=207/250$, so that $\kappa:=\cos^2\psi+\sin^2\psi=1-\frac{51}{62500}<1$. The weight is the column $k$ (with the signs of $x$ absorbed) mapped by the transformation $Z$ of \cref{fig:rotation}. Written out, and scaled by $\rho^2$ to avoid division, it is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight}
\tilde w^{(k)}=\bigl(\sin\psi\cdot\rho\, r_k,\ \ \rho^2M_{ik}+(c-\cos\psi)\,x_i\,r_k\ \ (i=1,\dots,4)\bigr).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[norm of the weight; in \lean{LemmaF5}{band_core}]\label{lem:norm}
If column $k$ is a unit vector orthogonal to column $j$, then
\[
\norm{\tilde w^{(k)}}_2^2=\rho^4-(1-\kappa)\,\rho^2r_k^2\ \le\ \rho^4 .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Expand. The relations $r_k^2+\sum_iM_{ik}^2=1$, $c\,r_k+\sum_ix_iM_{ik}=0$ and $c^2+\sum_ix_i^2=1=c^2+\rho^2$ reduce the sum of squares to $\rho^4+\rho^2r_k^2\bigl(\sin^2\psi-\rho^2-2c(c-\cos\psi)+(c-\cos\psi)^2\bigr)=\rho^4-\rho^2r_k^2(1-\kappa)$. In Lean this is one \code{linear_combination} with explicit polynomial coefficients.
\end{proof}

Applying the zero-sum bound with this weight and $K=\rho^2$ (\lean{LemmaF5}{zs_bound}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zs}
\tfrac{140}{99}\sum_{i}\abs{\zeta^{(k)}_i}\abs{\tilde w^{(k)}_i}\le\rho^2\norm{\zeta^{(k)}}_1 .
\end{equation}
The left side is bounded below without absolute values: the first term equals $c\sin\psi\,\rho\,r_k^2$, and $\abs{x_iM_{ik}}\abs{\tilde w_i}\ge\abs{x_i}M_{ik}\tilde w_i$ (\lean{LemmaF5}{abs_term}).

\begin{lemma}[sum over the columns; in \lean{LemmaF5}{band_core}]\label{lem:sum}
\[
\sum_{k}\Bigl(c\sin\psi\,\rho\,r_k^2+\sum_i\abs{x_i}M_{ik}\tilde w^{(k)}_i\Bigr)=c\sin\psi\,\rho^3+\rho^2\,(T-m_3)-c\,(c-\cos\psi)\,m_3 .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $\sum_kr_k^2=1-c^2=\rho^2$ (row $i_0$), $\sum_kM_{ik}^2=1-x_i^2$ (row $i$), and $\sum_kr_kM_{ik}=-c\,x_i$ (rows $i_0$ and $i$ orthogonal), and $\abs{x_i}x_i^2=X_i^3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The band; \lean{LemmaF5}{band_core}, \lean{LemmaF5}{band5}]\label{thm:band}
If $9/20\le c^2\le3/4$, then $F_1(c,r,x,M)\le1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $c^2+\sum_ix_i^2=1$, we have $F_1=2c+2T-1-\sum_kL_k$ with $L_k=\norm{\zeta^{(k)}}_1$. Summing \eqref{eq:zs} over $k$ and using \cref{lem:sum},
\[
\rho^2\sum_kL_k\ \ge\ \tfrac{140}{99}\bigl(c\sin\psi\,\rho^3+\rho^2T-\rho^2m_3-c(c-\cos\psi)m_3\bigr).
\]
So $F_1\le1$ follows from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:band}
\rho^2\,(2c+2T-2)\ \le\ \tfrac{140}{99}\bigl(c\sin\psi\,\rho^3+\rho^2T-\rho^2m_3-c(c-\cos\psi)m_3\bigr),
\end{equation}
which is \lean{LemmaF5Sphere}{band_final} (\cref{sec:sphere,sec:onevar}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reduction to a sphere inequality}\label{sec:sphere}
Write $r=140/99$ and $D(c)=r(1-c\cos\psi)$. Using $\rho^2+c^2=1$, \eqref{eq:band} reads
\[
(2-r)\,\rho^2T+D(c)\,m_3\ \le\ r\sin\psi\,c\,\rho^3+(2-2c)\,\rho^2 .
\]
Divide by $D(c)\rho^3$ and put $s_i=X_i/\rho$, a nonnegative unit vector in $\R^4$. The left side becomes $\rho^3D(c)\bigl(\lambda\sum_is_i+\sum_is_i^3\bigr)$ with
\[
\lambda=\lambda(c)=\frac{2-r}{D(c)}=\frac{29/70}{1-\frac{14}{25}c}\in[0.663,\,0.805]\qquad(9/20\le c^2\le3/4).
\]
So it suffices to bound $\sum_i(s_i^3+\lambda s_i)$ on the sphere.

\begin{definition}
For $\lambda>0$ let $\Psi_n(\lambda)=\max\bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^n(s_i^3+\lambda s_i):\ s\in\R^n_{\ge0},\ \norm s_2=1\bigr\}$.
\end{definition}

At a maximiser the Lagrange condition $3s_i+\lambda/s_i=2\nu$ has at most two positive solutions, so the nonzero $s_i$ take at most two values. Numerically the maximiser of $\Psi_4$ in the band has one large coordinate and three equal small ones, $(a,b,b,b)$, with $a^2$ from about $0.82$ at $\lambda=0.66$ down to $0.68$ at $\lambda=0.805$. Other two-level points come within $0.005$ to $0.04$ of the maximum, which is why the certificates need several intervals. Because $\sum_i(s_i^3+\lambda s_i)$ is affine in $\lambda$, bounds at two values $\lambda_a<\lambda_b$ give a bound on $[\lambda_a,\lambda_b]$ by interpolation. We certify three nodes.

\subsection{Certificates for the sphere bounds}\label{sec:certificates}
Each sphere bound in \cref{tab:sphere} is proved in three steps (\leanfile{LemmaF5Sphere}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The largest coordinate.} A tournament of comparisons reduces the statement to the case where $s_0$ is the largest coordinate (for instance \lean{LemmaF5Sphere}{sphA_core}). Then $s_0^2\ge1/n$, every other $s_i\le s_0$, and $s_i^2\le1/2$.
\item \textbf{Line bounds on intervals.} The ranges of $s_0$ and of the other coordinates are cut into intervals. On each interval a line in $s^2$ bounds $s^3+\lambda s$ from above, by an exact factorization of the gap:
\begin{gather*}
\text{tangent at }t:\quad \alpha+\beta s^2-s^3-\lambda s=(s-t)^2(u-s),\qquad u=\tfrac{\lambda-t^2}{2t},\ \ 0\le s\le u;\\
\text{chord on }[a,b]:\quad \alpha+\beta s^2-s^3-\lambda s=(s-a)(b-s)(s-u),\qquad u=\tfrac{\lambda-ab}{a+b}\le a\le s\le b.
\end{gather*}
All parameters are rational. \Cref{fig:sphere} shows the gaps for $\lambda=33/50$.
\item \textbf{Linear cases.} For each choice of intervals, the sum of the line bounds is linear in the $s_i^2$. With $\sum_is_i^2=1$, $s_i^2\le s_0^2$ and the interval endpoints, \code{linarith} closes it. In Lean the squares and cubes are first replaced by fresh variables, so that every case is purely linear.
\end{enumerate}
The partitions were found by a random search over breakpoints in which every candidate was scored by solving its case linear programs numerically (\codepath{scripts/optimality/lemma_f5/}). They are stored in \codepath{certificates.json}, from which \code{build.py} generates \leanfile{LemmaF5Sphere}. The search only proposes; Lean checks each line lemma and each case.

\subsection{The one-variable inequality}\label{sec:onevar}
Rescaling the sphere bounds to radius $\rho$ gives $\lambda_a\rho^2T+m_3\le U_a\rho^3$ (\lean{LemmaF5}{scale4}). For $\lambda(c)\in[\lambda_a,\lambda_b]$, take the nonnegative weights
\[
\alpha(c)=\frac{D(c)\lambda_b-(2-r)}{\lambda_b-\lambda_a},\qquad\beta(c)=\frac{(2-r)-D(c)\lambda_a}{\lambda_b-\lambda_a},
\]
so that $\alpha+\beta=D(c)$ and $\alpha\lambda_a+\beta\lambda_b=2-r$. Then $(2-r)\rho^2T+D(c)m_3\le\rho^3\bigl(\alpha U_a+\beta U_b\bigr)$, and \eqref{eq:band} follows from the one-variable inequality
\[
\rho\,G(c)\le 2-2c,\qquad G(c)=\alpha(c)U_a+\beta(c)U_b-r\sin\psi\,c,\qquad\rho=\sqrt{1-c^2}.
\]
$G$ is linear in $c$. The band is split at $c=11225/14406$, where $\lambda(c)=147/200$:
\begin{align*}
G_1(c)&=\tfrac{15338317}{7425000}-\tfrac{6108137}{3437500}\,c&&\text{on }[0.67,\,11225/14406],&&\text{\lean{LemmaF5Sphere}{seg1}}\\
G_2(c)&=\tfrac{14012927}{6930000}-\tfrac{21295267}{12375000}\,c&&\text{on }[11225/14406,\,0.8661],&&\text{\lean{LemmaF5Sphere}{seg2}}
\end{align*}
(the interval ends $0.67<\sqrt{9/20}$ and $0.8661>\sqrt{3/4}$ are rational). Squaring, $\rho G\le2-2c$ follows from the cubic inequality $(1+c)G(c)^2\le4(1-c)$, proved by \code{nlinarith} from products of the interval constraints. \Cref{fig:margin} shows the slack: it is smallest at $c^2=3/4$, about $0.002$.

\subsection{The far region}\label{sec:far5}
If every entry of column $j$ has square at most $9/20$, the argument of \cref{sec:d4} applies with five rows: \cref{lem:pair} for the four other columns (\lean{LemmaF5}{pair5}) gives $F_j\le 2S-1-r\sum_ix_i(1-x_i^2)$, and $F_j\le1$ reduces to
\[
\sum_{i=1}^5\Bigl(x_i^3+\tfrac{29}{70}x_i\Bigr)\le\tfrac{99}{70}\qquad\Bigl(\sum_ix_i^2=1,\ x_i^2\le\tfrac{9}{20}\Bigr),
\]
which is \lean{LemmaF5Sphere}{cs5} (\cref{tab:sphere}; true maximum $1.40607$, attained near $x=(0.659,0.659,0.210,0.210,0.210)$). See \lean{LemmaF5}{far5_core} and \lean{LemmaF5}{far5}.

\subsection{Lemma F in dimension 5}

\begin{theorem}[\lean{LemmaF5}{lemmaF5}]\label{thm:d5}
$F_j(R)\le1$ for every orthogonal $5\times5$ matrix and every column $j$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If some entry of column $j$ has square at least $3/4$, use \cref{thm:near} with $m=4$ and \cref{thm:d4} (\lean{LemmaF5}{near5}). Otherwise, if some entry has square at least $9/20$, use \cref{thm:band} (\lean{LemmaF5}{band5}). Otherwise every entry has square below $9/20$; use \lean{LemmaF5}{far5}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[\lean{Penteracts}{centre5}, \lean{Penteracts}{hc5inhc5_plateau}]
A unit five-cube inside a five-cube of side $s<2$ contains the centre. Side $2$ is optimal for every $2\le n\le32$ unit five-cubes.
\end{corollary}

Hosono~\cite{hosono} proved $s_5(2)=2$ first, with a spectral certificate of $21{,}502$ leaves for the region away from the axes.

\section{Outlook}\label{sec:outlook}

\paragraph{Dimension 6.} Hosono proved $s_6(2)=2$ with computer assistance, which settles the 23 records $n=2$--$24$ of the catalogue's six-cube family; they are not yet in Lean. Lemma~F in dimension~5 is now available as the input of the near-axis step, but that step uses $\norm{Q_i}_1\le2$ for the rows of a $5\times5$ orthogonal matrix, which fails (the bound is $\sqrt5$). A Lean proof in dimension~6 needs a different near-axis step, and new sphere bounds for the band and the far region. Paper~IV~\cite{alldims} supplies the near-axis step for the restricted lemma in every dimension, so only an explicit remaining case is left in dimension~$6$.

\paragraph{Lemma F in general.} Lemma~F is now proved in Lean for $d\le5$, and for $d=6$ with computer assistance~\cite{hosono}; whether it holds in every dimension is open. A proof for all $d$ would give $s_d(n)=2$ for all $2\le n\le 2^d$. Paper~IV of this series~\cite{alldims} collects what we know in every dimension. The cube centre lemma needs Lemma~F only for matrices whose rows have $\ell^1$ norm at most~$2$, and for those the near-axis step of \cref{sec:near} works in every dimension, with no condition on~$m$. With an elementary bound for the remaining columns, this gives a third proof for $d\le4$, without computation. Further steps reduce the induction on $d$ to an explicit remaining case. A new aligned step reduces the conjecture in every dimension, in Lean, to a geometric statement about the largest cube in a simplex with vertices on orthogonal axes (the facet lemma). The minimiser argument of \cref{sec:d3} is the case $d=3$ of that statement. Paper~IV also records the routes that fail, with verified counterexamples, and a numerical search in dimensions up to~$32$ that finds no counterexample.

\paragraph{Beyond the plateau.} In dimension~3, Hosono proved $s_3(9)=s_3(10)=2+1/\sqrt2$ and $s_3(25)=s_3(26)=3$ with interval branch and bound. These are the next targets for a Lean proof; they need lemmas about two or more pieces at once, not a single common point.
