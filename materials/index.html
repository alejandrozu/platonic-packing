Packing and materials science

A particle's shape helps determine its neighbours, its orientation and the spaces around it. Together, these geometric choices can change how a material assembles and what it does.

Polyhedral packing connects a mathematical question to a practical materials question: how do many faceted building blocks organize into a larger structure? Nanocrystals, colloidal particles and granular powders make that connection tangible. Their faces, edges and vertices affect possible contacts; a container adds walls and corners that compete with the arrangement preferred in the interior. Experiments and simulations show that these effects can select different crystals and orientation patterns. [1] [2] [8]

Shape and boundaries

Facets, rounding, particle number and the enclosing geometry constrain what can fit.

Collective structure

Orientations, neighbours, contacts, domains and connected voids describe the arrangement.

Physical response

Interactions and composition turn that structure into optical, mechanical or transport behaviour.

This site isolates the first two steps: it records explicit placements in finite containers, with attribution and exact geometric certificates where available. The studies below establish the physical relevance of these questions. Using this particular catalogue as a benchmark or a source of candidate structures is a research opportunity; a materials experiment or thermodynamic calculation is a further step.

Why faceted shape changes crystallization

For a sphere, rotating the particle leaves its occupied region unchanged. For a polyhedron, rotation changes which faces can approach neighbours and how much room remains for other particles to move. Even without attractive bonds, this difference can favour particular alignments: an ordered arrangement can provide more accessible configurations overall than a disordered one. This is the basis of shape-dependent entropic ordering in hard-particle models.

Simulation Damasceno, Engel and Glotzer studied 145 polyhedral shapes and found a wide range of self-assembled structures, including crystals with positional order, rotator phases with greater orientational freedom, and liquid-crystalline order. Hard tetrahedra also provide a striking example of complexity: simulations produced a dodecagonal quasicrystal as well as crystalline arrangements. A repeated particle shape therefore does not imply a single orientation or a simple repeating unit. [1] [3]

Experiment and simulation Henzie and colleagues assembled silver nanocubes, truncated cubes and octahedra into extended supercrystals. Polymer-coated particles could behave approximately as hard shapes; changing polymer-mediated attractions selected a different octahedral superstructure. This is a direct demonstration that polyhedral geometry matters in a real material, while surface chemistry still helps determine which arrangement appears. [2]

The smallest possible container is a valuable geometric reference, but assembly at finite pressure is governed by free energy and accessible motion. Cersonsky and colleagues explicitly tested the relationship and showed why a densest packing does not, by itself, predict the thermodynamically preferred shape or explain self-assembly. Geometry supplies candidates and constraints; entropy, interactions and kinetics select among them. [4]

Why the container and particle count matter

A finite assembly has an interior and a boundary. Near a wall, some neighbour positions are unavailable and certain faces can align particularly well. At an edge or corner, several boundary preferences meet. As particle count changes, an arrangement may accommodate another layer, reorganize its interior or sacrifice local density to fit the boundary. This is why changing the enclosing shape can change the relevant packing problem.

Finite-cluster simulation Teich and colleagues studied 4–60 copies of each Platonic solid in spherical confinement. Their dense clusters showed layers, preferred particle counts and shape-dependent symmetries; finite-container behaviour could differ from bulk packing. Later work on confined tetrahedral shapes found boundary-induced motifs persisting in much larger assemblies. These spherical-container results motivate comparisons with polyhedral containers, whose flat faces and corners impose different constraints. [5] [22]

Experiment and simulation Rounded nanocubes in shrinking droplets formed structures different from those of more sharply faceted cubes. In another study, gold nanorods and bipyramids assembled in cavities with triangular, square, pentagonal and hexagonal cross sections: the mold walls selected differently oriented domains. These are particularly direct demonstrations that both particle shape and boundary shape can reorganize an assembly. [6] [8]

Confinement can also change which phases are accessible. Simulations of several polyhedra between parallel plates found layering, buckling and orientationally disordered phases as plate spacing changed. Microscopy of truncated tetrahedral microparticles under quasi-two-dimensional confinement directly observed a gravity-driven structural transition involving particle rotations initiated at crystalline defects. These systems have different boundaries and physical protocols from this site's closed 3D containers, but establish why permitted motion and available space deserve explicit study. [7] [9]

In the catalogue, scale jumps, plateaus and changes of orientation are therefore useful places to inspect coordinates and look for competing motifs. A jump in the best construction found so far is not sufficient evidence of a physical phase transition, a completed shell or a jump in the unknown optimum. Those interpretations require structural analysis, comparable search effort and, for physical claims, an assembly model.

Orientations, domains and crystal grains

Several distinct levels of order matter. A particle has a pose. Many particles may form a spatially connected domain with common local alignment and positional order. The lattice of that collective assembly can itself be oriented relative to another domain. Inside a crystalline nanoparticle there is also an atomic lattice. These orientations need not be interchangeable.

Experiment Nanodiffraction of lead-sulfide nanocrystal superlattices resolved changes in lattice spacing and collective orientation near grain boundaries while tracking the nanocrystals' orientation relative to the superlattice. The prismatic-confinement experiments likewise found domains seeded by different mold walls meeting at boundaries. They show why both the spatial arrangement and crystallographic orientation are needed to characterize a grain boundary. [11] [8]

The viewer's orientation colours help reveal alignment and competing motifs. They do not on their own identify crystal grains: a grain is an extended ordered region, and disconnected pieces sharing a colour need not belong to one. Symmetry-equivalent poses, neighbour relationships and spatial correlations should be considered before assigning domains.

There is also a broader connection to metals and ceramics. Their crystal grains form a space-filling microstructure whose boundaries move as grains grow and shrink. MacPherson and Srolovitz derived a geometric relation for three-dimensional capillarity-driven coarsening; atomistic calculations by Olmsted and colleagues show that grain-boundary energy depends on boundary crystallography. Such grains can change shape and exchange volume. Rigid identical polyhedra in a container provide a controlled geometric analogy, rather than a full model of that growth process. [12] [13]

Why arrangement affects useful properties

Two arrangements can have similar occupied volume yet different contacts, orientation patterns and connected voids. These features are important because a material responds through its structure: electromagnetic fields couple across gaps, forces travel through contacts, and molecules can enter only accessible pores. The relevant objective may be a particular structure or response, rather than maximum density.

Confinement also changes the coordination and density of protocol-generated granular packings. Here “grain” means a powder particle, rather than an extended crystalline domain. At larger scales, Santos and colleagues assembled faceted nanoparticle-superlattice crystallites into macroscopic solids while retaining nanoscale ordering. That hierarchical result illustrates why particle arrangement, domain structure and processing all matter when moving from an attractive microscopic geometry to an actual bulk material. [21]

What can be proved in Lean?

Two formal developments connect the geometric certificates to classical ingredients of materials models. They concern the allowed configurations and repetition of ideal rigid bodies, with the assumptions stated explicitly.

  • A certified region of motion. If the wall and pair clearances satisfy a positive error budget ε, every particle may translate independently within a coordinate box of radius ε, with all particles moving simultaneously. With orientations fixed, the product Lebesgue volume of this safe region is at least (2ε)nd for n labelled particles in dimension d. Inspect the Lean source.
  • A repeatable periodic motif. A nonoverlapping finite motif lying wholly inside an open rectangular cell can be repeated at every integer cell translation without overlaps. The proof connects finite certificates to a global periodic family. It is a sufficient condition: general motifs crossing cell faces need extra checks against periodic images. Inspect the Lean source.

Configuration volume and periodic motifs are established concepts in hard-particle statistical mechanics and crystallographic modelling. The new work formalizes these implications from explicit geometric conditions. A simultaneous safe box is a rigorous feasible subset; it does not assume that the full configuration region factorizes into independent single-particle volumes. [23] [24] [25]

These proofs do not supply a full rotational partition function, predict an alloy's properties or prove that a periodic arrangement will crystallize. A polyhedral container also need not tile space. Read the GitHub explanation, assumptions and reproduction guide, with links to the proof evidence and the updated generalization manuscript.

How this catalogue can support materials research

The contribution is a common geometric reference: explicit particle positions and rotations, stated container geometry, construction provenance, and exact containment and separation checks for certified witnesses. This makes candidate structures inspectable and comparable across shapes and particle counts. The proposed uses below are opportunities for further research, rather than measurements already made by the site.

  • Compare confinement systematically. Hold the piece shape and particle count fixed while changing the container. Inspect wall alignment, corner occupation, interior motifs and the volume-normalized density described in the method guide.
  • Investigate structural changes. Compare neighbouring particle counts, alternative constructions and orientation patterns. Measure local order and neighbour correlations to test whether a scale jump accompanies a layer change or a competing motif.
  • Supply reproducible candidate geometries. Use native coordinates to initialize a physical simulation, then add the appropriate temperature, pressure, surface interactions, solvent or friction. Relaxing a geometric candidate and comparing its free energy or dynamics tests whether it is physically accessible.
  • Study contacts and voids. Derive near-neighbour networks and accessible pore geometry from the exact witness, with a stated probe size or distance threshold. Certified records can have small positive gaps, so visual near-contact is not itself an exact mechanical contact.
  • Explore shape design. Inverse-design simulations have optimized hard-particle shapes to assemble target crystals. A geometry catalogue can suggest motifs and comparison cases for that separate thermodynamic task. [20]

There is room to extend these questions to mixtures. Work on confined tetrahedron–octahedron mixtures found improved crystalline order under selected conditions, without lowering the minimum packing fraction for crystallization. This is a useful direction for future research, and a different model from the current records of identical pieces inside each container. [10]

The catalogue's 4D–6D polytopes extend the mathematics and algorithms. Their higher-dimensional coordinates are not additional spatial dimensions of an ordinary material. Connections to a physical system require an explicit model.

Start with the interactive records, download a native witness from all constructions, or read what its certificate proves. Materials researchers are welcome to contribute constructions or propose a relevant 3D category, preserving source attribution and the physical assumptions needed for comparison.
